Section 01

The problem, and what a "fold" is

The system is given a photograph of a fruit bat's face and has to decide whether two photographs show the same individual bat. Not the same species — the same animal. Two species are in play: mauritius (16 individuals, 520 images) and rousettus (12 individuals, 1092 images).

The hard constraint that shapes everything downstream is that this is an open-set problem: the bats we test on must be bats the model has never seen. Anything else would be measuring the wrong thing.

Concept

Open-set recognition, and why splits must be identity-disjoint

In an ordinary classification problem you decide among a fixed set of categories that were all present during training. That is closed-set. Here the useful question is different: shown a bat the model has never encountered, can it still tell it apart from another new bat?

So the data is partitioned by individual, not by image. Every image of bat arrowhead is in exactly one of train, validation, or test — never split across them. That is what identity-disjoint means. If even one of arrowhead's photographs leaked into training while others sat in test, the model could recognise that specific bat rather than having learned to compare faces, and the score would be meaningless — inflated by memorisation.

Concept

ROC-AUC — the number almost everything here is measured in

Given a pair of images the model outputs a similarity score. ROC-AUC answers: if I draw one genuine pair (same bat) and one impostor pair (different bats) at random, what is the probability the genuine pair gets the higher score?

  • 1.0 — perfect: every genuine pair scores above every impostor pair.
  • 0.5 — pure chance, equivalent to a coin flip. This is the floor that matters.
  • Below 0.5 — actively wrong, which does occur in the weakest cells here.

Its useful property is that it needs no decision threshold — it only cares about ranking. That is exactly why it became the primary metric later on.

A "fold" is one particular way of dealing out the bats

With only 12–16 individuals, which bats land in the test set dominates the result. Hold out two easily-distinguished bats and the score looks excellent; hold out two similar ones and it collapses. A single split therefore tells you very little.

A fold is one deal of the cards: one assignment of individuals to train / validation / test. Twenty folds means twenty different deals, which lets us report a range rather than one lucky or unlucky number.

Section 03

Are the two datasets even comparable?

This had to come first. If we compare mauritius against rousettus and mauritius wins, there are two possible explanations: those bats really are more distinguishable, or their photographs are simply better. Until that is settled, every species comparison is uninterpretable.

Concept

Confounding

A confounder is a third factor that moves with the thing you are measuring and offers a rival explanation for your result. Classic example: ice-cream sales predict drownings — the confounder is summer.

Here the candidate confounder is image quality. The two species were filmed by different people, in different places, under different light. If those photographs differ systematically, a performance gap tells you about cameras and circumstances, not about bats.

Concept

Pseudoreplication — why 1612 images are really only 28 data points

We have 520 + 1092 = 1612 images, which sounds like plenty of statistical power. It isn't. All images of one bat come from one video of one animal: consecutive frames are near-duplicates. They are not independent observations of "a mauritius bat"; they are repeated looks at the same one.

Treating them as independent is pseudoreplication, and it manufactures confidence out of nothing. A per-image test on this data returns p < 1e−30 for almost any comparison you care to run — which is a symptom, not a discovery.

The fix used throughout this report: collapse each individual to a single number (its median), then compare. That leaves 16 versus 12 data points. Far less impressive, and honest.

Concept

Effect size — because a p-value alone cannot tell you if something matters

A p-value answers "could this be chance?" It says nothing about how big the difference is. With enough data, a trivial difference becomes "significant"; with little data, a huge one may not be. So every test here is paired with an effect size.

  • Cliff's δ (delta) — see the worked example below; it measures how consistently one group outranks the other on one specific measure.
  • Hedges' g — the difference between the two group means measured in standard deviations, with a correction for small samples. Used here to define the equivalence margin below.

Concept — worked example

Cliff's δ: higher in what?

In whatever quantity that row of the table is measuring. δ is always "δ for some measure" — it has no meaning detached from one. Every row in the table below has its own δ, and the sign can flip between rows:

  • native_side_px, δ = +1.00 → every mauritius bat has a larger face in the frame than every rousettus bat.
  • rms_contrast, δ = −0.48 → mauritius tends to be lower in contrast. Same statistic, opposite direction, because the measured quantity changed.

Positive always means mauritius-higher here, because mauritius is passed as the first group.

The mechanics, on three bats per species. Suppose you measured face resolution:

Compare every mauritius bat against every rousettus bat — 3 × 3 = 9 pairs. Mauritius wins all 9, so δ = (9 − 0) / 9 = +1.00.

Now make one rousettus bat unusually well-resolved — say 650 instead of 400. Mauritius now wins 7 pairs and loses 2 (both 500 and 600 lose to 650), giving δ = (7 − 2) / 9 = +0.56.

Formally δ = P(mauritius higher) − P(mauritius lower). It is the difference between those two proportions, so 70% wins against 30% losses gives 0.4, not 0.7.

Why this statistic: δ counts only which value is bigger, never by how much. That makes it immune to outliers and to odd distribution shapes — appropriate for 16 vs 12 hand-measured animals. Rough reading: 0.15 negligible, 0.33 small, 0.47 medium, above that large.

And why ±1.00 is a different kind of claim: it says the groups do not overlap at all, which is far stronger than a big average gap. Contrast the sharpness row (δ = +0.74, genuinely large) — there, some rousettus bats do out-sharpen some mauritius bats; the distributions are merely shifted. Resolution is the confound worth worrying about precisely because it is the one where they are completely disjoint.

How it differs from Cliff's δ. δ counts only rankings; g uses magnitudes. They can therefore disagree — a single wild outlier inflates g while barely moving δ. Reporting both is a cheap safeguard: agreement means the finding is solid, divergence points at an outlier or an odd distribution shape.

Concept

Why "no significant difference" is not a finding — and what TOST does about it

This is the single most important statistical idea in this section, so here is the machinery from the start.

You never prove a claim directly. You write down a statement you hope to knock down — the null hypothesis — then ask: if that statement were true, how surprising would my data be? That surprise is the p-value. Small p means the data would be very unlikely under the null, so you reject it. Large p means the data is unremarkable under the null, so you fail to reject.

The asymmetry is the whole point: rejecting gives you a conclusion; failing to reject gives you nothing. "I couldn't knock it down" is not evidence the statement is true — you might simply have had too little data to knock anything down.

So whatever you actually want to claim must be placed in the slot that gets rejected, never the slot that survives. Compare the two setups:

 Standard testTOST (equivalence)
Null hypothesis (the thing you try to knock down) "the two are identical" "the two differ by at least X"
If you reject it ✓ conclude "they differ" ✓ conclude "they are the same, to within X"
If you fail ✗ no conclusion — not "they're the same" ✗ no conclusion — a real difference can't be ruled out

Read the top-right cell against the bottom-left one. In a standard test, "they are the same" sits in the slot that can never yield a conclusion — which is why comment 1 was structurally unanswerable that way, no matter how much data we collected. TOST swaps what occupies the null slot, so a successful rejection now delivers "equivalent" rather than "different". Same machinery, opposite question.

Why "two one-sided tests". There are two ways to fail to be equivalent: the difference could be far below −X, or far above +X. Both have to be ruled out, so you run one test against each bound and both must pass. The reported p-value is the larger of the two — the weaker link. Hence the name.

You must declare X in advance — the largest difference you would consider unimportant. We set |g| < 0.5: "differences smaller than half a between-bat standard deviation don't matter for our purposes". Choosing X after seeing the data would be circular.

What happened here. Every measure came back with pTOST ≥ 0.53, nowhere near the 0.05 needed. For rms_contrast you can see why without any statistics: g = −0.51 is already larger than the ±0.5 margin, so equivalence was never in reach.

The answer to comment 1

Eleven of twelve measures differ significantly, and not one is equivalent under the declared margin — every TOST p-value was ≥ 0.53, nowhere near the 0.05 needed to claim sameness. The one non-significant measure (tenengrad) is inconclusive, not equivalent: under-powered at 16 vs 12 bats. The datasets are not comparable, so the species comparison must be controlled rather than assumed clean.

The dominant difference is resolution, and it separates the species completely

The bats were not photographed from the same distance. A mauritius face occupies a median 614 pixels in the original frame; a rousettus face 312. Both are then resized to the same 224-pixel input, so one is being downsampled from real detail while the other is interpolated up from less.

Cliff's δ here is +1.00 — not "large", but total. Every single mauritius bat is better-resolved than every single rousettus bat; the distributions do not overlap at all (mauritius minimum 441 px, rousettus maximum 407 px). At 320-pixel input, 48.4% of rousettus crops are being invented by interpolation versus 0.2% of mauritius.

A surprise: mauritius images are noisier, not cleaner

Two independent noise estimators agree that mauritius images carry 2.2–3.1× more noise. That matters because the headline "sharpness" measure — variance of the Laplacian — cannot tell fine detail from noise. Both are high-frequency energy. So part of that dramatic 3.78× sharpness advantage is not detail at all; it is sensor noise being counted as signal.

The three sharpness measures also disagree in a revealing pattern. Ordered by how sensitive each is to scale: gradient_energy 2.08×, laplacian_var 3.78×, and tenengrad — the one least fooled by noise — only 1.19× and not significant. A genuine difference in focus would move all three together. This pattern is the signature of a resolution difference, not a focus difference.

And rousettus is not simply the worse dataset: it has higher contrast on both measures and marginally more information content. The two datasets differ along several axes in opposing directions.

Does the quality gap actually change what a model can learn?

Quality measures describe the photographs. The question that matters for recognition is whether the individuals are distinguishable. That has a natural framing as signal versus noise.

Concept

Signal and noise, for identity

Signal = how much bats differ from each other. Noise = how much one bat's own photographs differ from one another. Their ratio is the intrinsic difficulty of the dataset. Three standard ways to quantify it:

  • d′ (d-prime) — how far apart the "same bat" and "different bat" similarity distributions sit, measured in their own spread. Bigger is better.
  • Fisher ratio — between-bat scatter divided by within-bat scatter.
  • Silhouette — how cleanly each bat's images cluster away from other bats'.

Crucially these were measured in a frozen, off-the-shelf ImageNet network — never a model trained on these bats. That makes the answer a property of the datasets, not of our architecture choices.

Concept — a method choice worth understanding

Why those intervals are jackknives, not bootstraps

To put an uncertainty range on a statistic you resample your data many times and watch how much the answer moves. The usual method is the bootstrap: draw bats with replacement, so the same bat can appear twice.

That silently breaks clustering measures. If bat gini is drawn twice, it becomes two clusters sitting on top of each other — artificially perfect overlap. Silhouette and Fisher ratio collapse. We caught this because the reported confidence interval did not contain its own point estimate (silhouette 0.246 with an interval of [−0.006, 0.171]) — an impossible result that is a reliable signal something is wrong.

The fix was a delete-2 jackknife: instead of duplicating bats, remove two and recompute, across many combinations. Every cluster stays distinct, and the intervals became sane.

Section 04

Building folds that vary honestly

Before this work the project already had 90 runs described as "multi-seed". Inspecting them revealed that the seed varied only the training randomness — the neural network's weight initialisation and batch order. The data split was frozen: every one of those runs held out the same two bats.

That measures the wrong kind of variability. It tells you how much the answer wobbles if you retrain on identical data, which is small and uninteresting. It tells you nothing about the thing that actually dominates: which bats you happened to hold out.

What changed

The split is now re-drawn from the seed, in memory, at the start of each run — and the number of held-out bats varies too. test_fraction became a floor rather than an exact target: the seed draws the actual count within declared bounds (mauritius 2–6 bats, rousettus 2–5). The old fixed-size behaviour is preserved as a separate mode so the 90 published runs remain exactly reproducible.

Letting the size vary was a deliberate choice, and it is what makes the fourth result in section 06 possible: with a fixed test size you can never ask how many held-out bats do we actually need.

Reproducibility is the whole point

A split that exists only in memory is worthless unless it can be rebuilt years later. Each run therefore records the seed, the sizing mode, the bounds, the realised counts, a digest of the exact bat-to-split assignment, and the hash of the source data file — plus the full identity lists as an attached artifact.

One subtlety worth recording: the data file's hash includes the split column, so re-splitting changes it. Logging the new hash would have quietly broken comparability with every earlier run, so the original on-disk hash is what gets logged, with the re-split assignment tracked separately.

A correctness check that turned out to matter

Across the finished sweep there are exactly 40 distinct split assignments — 2 species × 20 folds. That confirms all nine model/background combinations within a fold see the identical partition. Without that property the model-versus-model comparisons in section 06 would have had to be unpaired, at a large cost in statistical power.

Section 05

Fixing the statistics

Comment 4 asked for a p-value per species. The project already produced them — but three things were wrong, and one of them meant the headline claim in the results report was not supported by what had actually been computed.

Concept

What a p-value is, and what it is not

A p-value is the probability of seeing a result at least this extreme if nothing real were going on. Small p means "chance alone would rarely produce this".

It is not the probability that your hypothesis is true, and it is not a measure of how large or important the effect is. That is why every p in this report travels with an effect size.

Concept

The permutation test — and why it has a hard floor

Rather than assume a mathematical form for "nothing going on", a permutation test constructs it empirically. Take the real predictions, shuffle the labels so any genuine association is destroyed, and re-score. Do that 1000 times and you have 1000 scores from a world where the model knows nothing. Then ask: where does the real score sit in that distribution?

The p-value is (number of shuffles that beat the real score + 1) / (1000 + 1). The +1 on both sides keeps the test valid and stops p from ever being exactly zero.

This creates a resolution floor. With 1000 shuffles the smallest achievable value is 1/1001 ≈ 0.000999. A run reported at that value did not "score 0.001" — it beat every shuffle, and its true p could be anything at or below the floor. It is censored, and quoting it as a point estimate claims precision that does not exist. In our sweep 290 of 360 runs sit exactly on the floor.

The defect that mattered most

ROC-AUC was never actually permuted. The permutation test covered only threshold-based metrics — F1, accuracy, precision, recall. But ROC-AUC is the number the project reports everywhere and promotes models on. So the results report's claim that "the observed ROC-AUC beats every shuffle" was attributing F1's p-values to a metric that had never been tested.

ROC-AUC is now permuted on every run and is the pre-declared primary metric. The report text was corrected in three places and the PDF rebuilt.

Concept

Multiple comparisons, and Benjamini–Hochberg

Run one test at the 0.05 level and you accept a 5% chance of a false alarm. Run eighteen tests and you should expect roughly one false alarm even if nothing whatsoever is going on. Report only the smallest p and you are guaranteed to mislead.

The project was doing a version of this: the summary script took min() across five correlated metrics per run — the best of five tries, presented as one result. That is now replaced by one metric declared in advance.

For the family of 18 cells (2 species × 3 models × 3 backgrounds) we apply Benjamini–Hochberg. It sorts the p-values and rescales each by its rank, producing adjusted values (often called q-values) that control the false discovery rate — the expected share of your "discoveries" that are false. It is gentler than demanding every single test be individually airtight, which is the right trade when you are screening many cells.

Practical consequence: a raw p of 0.046 among 3 tests can become q ≈ 0.14 — no longer significant. That happened to the saliency result in section 07, and it is reported as such.

Concept

Combining 20 p-values into one — and why not Fisher's method

Each cell now has 20 p-values, one per fold. To make a single statement per cell they must be combined.

The textbook tool is Fisher's method, but it assumes the tests are independent. Our folds are not: they share training data (see the next concept). Feeding dependent tests to Fisher makes it anti-conservative — it reports more significance than the evidence supports.

So the default here is the harmonic mean p-value (Wilson, 2019), which tolerates dependence between tests. Implementing it correctly required the Landau distribution; the implementation was verified against the reference package's published worked example, reproducing it to six significant figures.

Concept — the one you asked about

Nadeau–Bengio: why 20 folds are not 20 independent experiments

This is the correction that changes a headline conclusion, so it is worth getting clear.

Ordinary statistics assume your measurements are independent. Cross-validation folds are not. Fold 1 and fold 2 train on largely the same bats — with 12 individuals and 8 in training, two folds might share 6 or 7 of them. If a particular bat is unusually easy, that helps both folds. The scores move together.

Positively correlated measurements are less informative than independent ones, but the standard formula for uncertainty does not know that. It divides by the number of folds as though all 20 were fresh evidence, so it computes a variance that is too small, confidence intervals that are too narrow, and p-values that are too small. You get false positives — "model A beats model B" when the data cannot support it.

Nadeau & Bengio (2003) derived the repair: inflate the variance by a factor that depends on the ratio of test-set size to training-set size. Concretely the usual s²/n becomes (1/n + n_test/n_train) · s². The correction is conservative by construction — it always widens the interval, never narrows it.

It applies to paired comparisons that share folds, which is why the 40-distinct-assignments check in section 04 mattered. It does not apply across species, which cannot share folds because they are different animals; there we use a resampling comparison instead.

Section 06

The 360-run sweep and its four results

Two species × three models × three backgrounds × twenty folds = 360 training runs, every one with its own re-drawn split and its own 1000-shuffle permutation test. All 18 cells came back complete, with one run needing a re-run after a genuine bug (section 09).

Result 1 — the recognition is real, in every single cell

Combining each cell's 20 fold-level permutation p-values with the harmonic-mean method, then applying Benjamini–Hochberg across all 18, every cell is above chance (q ≤ 0.0032). This is the direct answer to comment 4.

Two cells illustrate why the machinery was needed. rousettus / ArcFace / random has a median per-fold p of 0.170 — on its own, entirely unconvincing. Combining twenty folds brings it to q = 0.0032. Twenty weak-but-consistent signals amount to real evidence, and the combination step is what lets you say so without overclaiming from any single fold.

Result 2 — the species gap is confined to the random background

Comparing species per cell (unpaired, since the animals differ), only two of nine combinations survive correction, and both are the randomised background: AdaFace (δ = +0.63, q = 0.006) and ArcFace (δ = +0.48, q = 0.044). On green and original, six of nine are negligible-to-small and none survive.

So there is no general "mauritius is easier" effect. The gap appears specifically where the background is replaced with random imagery — the condition that most stresses a model's ability to ignore context and attend to the face.

And it is still confounded

Section 03 established that the datasets differ on 11 of 12 quality measures with non-overlapping effective resolution. So even these two significant cells compare mauritius-dataset against rousettus-dataset, not one species against the other. The resolution-matched arm remains a prerequisite before attributing any of it to the animals. The analysis script prints this caveat alongside the numbers so it cannot be quoted without it.

Result 3 — no model is demonstrably better than another

ArcFace and AdaFace lead the Siamese network by up to +0.21 ROC-AUC, which looks decisive. After the Nadeau–Bengio correction, not one of the 18 within-cell model comparisons reaches significance — every p ≥ 0.24.

This is the correction earning its keep. A naive paired t-test on the same numbers would have declared several of these differences real. Twenty correlated folds over 12–16 bats simply cannot resolve a 0.1–0.2 difference when the fold-to-fold spread is itself 0.13–0.21. The honest statement is "consistent with ArcFace/AdaFace being better, but not established".

Result 4 — the variance surprise, which contradicts the premise of the whole exercise

The reasoning behind "do 20 folds" was: the 2-bat test set is noisy, so average over many folds and the noise washes out. Letting the test size vary let us check that assumption directly — and it does not hold.

Concept

Variance and standard deviation — what "spread" is telling you

The standard deviation (std) of the 20 fold scores measures how much the answer jumps around when you change which bats are held out. It is not a measurement error in the ordinary sense; it is real sensitivity of the result to the choice of test animals.

A mean of 0.89 with std 0.10 means individual folds routinely landed near 0.79 or 0.99. If you had run one fold and reported it, you could have been off by that much. This is why a single number was never trustworthy here — and why the size of the std is itself a finding.

The intuition being tested: more held-out bats should mean a more stable estimate, because you are averaging over more animals. That intuition assumes the extra bats add independent information.

The split shape barely predicts the score at all: regressing ROC-AUC on the number of held-out and training bats gives R² = 0.042 (mauritius) and 0.003 (rousettus) — essentially no explanatory power. Pooled std stays around 0.16 regardless.

The interpretation: the instability is not sampling noise that averaging away fixes. It comes from which bats are held out, not how many. Some individuals are simply much harder than others, and a test set containing a hard one scores badly whether it holds 2 bats or 6. Adding more bats adds more hard-bat lottery, not more precision.

Concept — the two caveats you asked about

Uneven fold counts, and why the two axes cannot be separated

Uneven counts. The levels do not carry equal weight: mauritius has 63 folds at 3 held-out bats but only 18 at 4; rousettus has 90 at 3 and 27 at 4. This happened because the size was drawn randomly per fold, so some sizes came up more often. The consequence is that each point on that chart is estimated with different precision — the std at 18 folds is a shakier number than the std at 90. The unusually low mauritius point at 4 bats (0.075) rests on the fewest folds of any level, so it is the one to trust least. Nothing is invalid; the points are just not equally reliable, and a weighted analysis would give the sparse levels less say.

The anti-correlation. The pool of bats is fixed, so every bat moved into the test set is one removed from training. The two quantities are mechanically anti-correlated: −0.83 (mauritius) and −0.87 (rousettus). That means a drift in the score can never be cleanly attributed to test size or training size — they move together by construction. Both coefficients are reported, and the honest claim is about "the shape of the split" as a whole, not about test size specifically.

Section 07

Where the models actually look

Comment 3 asked for kernel-smoothed saliency showing each species' focus. The cosmetic part was already shipped. The useful part — a per-species map plus numbers you can test — was not, and building it turned up a measurement problem that invalidated the first attempt.

Concept

Grad-CAM, and the resolution limit nobody mentions

Grad-CAM asks which regions of an image a network relied on, by checking which parts of its last convolutional layer most influenced the output. The answer arrives at the resolution of that layer's grid, not the input image — and for a ResNet50 the grid is the input size divided by 32.

That is decisive here. At the project's usual 112-pixel input the grid is 4 × 4: each cell covers a 56-pixel square, which is larger than the entire eye region we want to test. At 320 pixels the grid is 10 × 10 and cells are 32 pixels — fine enough to resolve an eye.

At 112 px every measurement came out at chance for both species. That was not a finding about bats; it was a map too coarse to say anything. The giveaway was a visible 4 × 4 blocking pattern in the per-pixel variability image.

What the 320-pixel measurement shows

Concept — worked example

"Density" — attention compared against fair share

A saliency map gives every pixel an importance value. Divide the whole map by its own total and it becomes a share: 100% of the model's attention, spread over the picture.

Now ask how much of that share lands on the eyes. On its own the answer is uninterpretable — of course a bigger region collects more attention. So it is measured against what the region would get if attention were sprinkled evenly, which is simply its share of the area.

the two eye discs cover16.1% of the picture
if attention were spread evenly they would collect16.1% of it
mauritius eyes actually collect26.8%

So 1.0 is exactly fair share. 1.67 means the eyes soak up 67% more attention than their size entitles them to. 0.91 — rousettus — means slightly less than fair share. The number is a ratio, so it has no units and can be compared across regions of different sizes.

One property worth knowing, because it constrains how the table can be read: eyes, nose and periphery partition the picture exactly. So the area-weighted average of the three densities is always 1.0, and the eyes cannot rise without the periphery falling. The three numbers are not independent measurements.

Concept

Density is not the same question as the pointing game

Two different measures appear in this section and they can move in opposite directions without either being wrong.

  • Pointing game — "where is the single hottest pixel?" One winner per image, and the rest of the map is ignored entirely.
  • Density — "how much of the total attention landed here?" Every pixel counts.

For the trained mauritius model the peak moved onto an eye (64% → 83% of images) while eye density barely moved (1.77 → 1.67). Both are true: the map became more sharply peaked at an eye without the eye region gaining any overall share. Density is the measure this project tests on, because the pointing game reduces a whole map to one pixel — and because two runs of the identical command differed by 6 percentage points on it before the underlying randomness was seeded.

Concept

Why the control is decisive, and how the first version of it lied

Saliency maps look persuasive even for models that have learned nothing, so any claim about what a model learned has to clear a randomisation control (Adebayo et al. 2018): re-run everything on an untrained network of identical architecture.

The first attempt at that control reported "100% of maps degenerate, nothing survives" and was written up as a decisive pass. It was worthless. The randomisation zeroed every 1-D parameter, which includes each BatchNorm's scale γ — and with γ = 0 the BatchNorm output is identically zero, so the network emitted nothing at all. It demonstrated a broken model, not a learning-dependent explanation. A control that can only ever return "empty" tests nothing.

The tell was visible and went unnoticed: the trained IG run produced 0% degenerate maps while the randomised arm produced 100%. Integrated Gradients has no ReLU gate — that is precisely why it removed the degenerate maps to begin with — so only a dead network could do that. Building the model at its default initialisation (γ = 1) instead of overwriting weights fixes it, and the honest control then fails.

Still open on this section

Both checkpoints are original-background models, and section 08 shows that arm is substantially solvable from the scene alone. A model partly scoring pairs on background may distribute attention differently from one forced onto the face, so this whole measurement should be repeated on green before anything is leaned on. Also, mauritius used a best-ROC-AUC checkpoint while rousettus never improved val ROC-AUC at 320 px and fell back to best-F1 — so the two arms were not selected on the same criterion.

Frequently misread

Does the mauritius model "look at the eyes" to tell its bats apart?

Not in the way the headline number suggests. The measure is the pointing game: for each image, does the single hottest attribution pixel fall inside an eye? For the trained mauritius model it does in 82.8% of images. Read alone, that looks like "the model learned to use the eyes".

But an untrained network of the same architecture — random weights, has learned nothing at all — scores 64.1% on the same images. Gradient attribution is drawn to high-contrast structure, and an eye is a dark spot on a lighter face. So most of the eye-peaking is a property of the photographs, not of anything the model learned. Only the +18.7 percentage points on top of 64.1% is attributable to training.

And the second eye measure disagrees. Eye density — how much attribution mass per unit area lands in the eye region — goes 1.771 untrained → 1.669 trained, i.e. training moves attention very slightly away. One metric says toward, the other says away.

So the defensible statement is narrow: training relocates the single strongest piece of evidence onto an eye in about 19% more mauritius images than an untrained network manages, and off the eye in about 33% more rousettus images. That is what "opposite directions" means. It is not "mauritius uses eyes and rousettus does not", and the earlier form of the claim — 81% against a 16% chance rate — is refuted, because 16% is the eye region's share of the crop area, which is the wrong null. The right null is 64%.

Three further caveats: this is a single seed, it runs on the original background that section 09 shows leaks, and the rousettus checkpoint at this resolution was picked by a fallback criterion because it never improved on the metric that matters.

What this cost, and what it bought

Five things worth carrying to the next analysis

1 · A single trained model is not evidence about interpretability. Going from one model to ten flipped the sign of the primary effect. The per-fold mauritius nose contrast on green ranged from +0.046 to −0.586 — the spread across training runs is larger than the effect being measured. Published saliency work routinely shows maps from one checkpoint; on this data that would have been enough to state a confident falsehood.

2 · The choice of metric mattered as much as the number of models. Re-running the published analysis on the identical checkpoint and identical images, changing only the random noise draw, moved the pointing game by 9.4 percentage points while density moved by 0.002. The effect under investigation was ~19 points. Excluding the pointing game in advance was not fastidiousness — it cannot measure something this size.

3 · A data leak contaminates interpretability, not just accuracy. Section 09 showed the original background is 93% solvable with the face deleted. The eye effect exists on original and vanishes on green — so the leak shaped where the model looked, not merely how well it scored. Every interpretability result computed on original-background checkpoints inherits that, including all of rounds 1 and 2.

4 · Every reversal in this project came from a control, not from more data. The randomisation control, the negative control, the pre-registration. None of them needed another bat, and each one shrank a claim that looked solid. That is the cheapest experimental capacity available here, given that the number of animals is fixed.

5 · Writing the hypothesis down first is what made the negative result publishable. The nose signal was genuinely the most impressive thing in the file — the species gap growing under training while the eyes' stayed flat. Had it been written up from the seed it was found in, the paper would carry a claim that ten folds contradict. Because it was registered first, with its failure condition named, "it reversed" is a finding rather than an embarrassment.

So what can be said about the biology?

Not much, honestly, and that is the correct answer rather than a gap to paper over. The hypothesis that mauritius individuals are distinguished by eye and nose features is not refuted — but every measurement that appeared to support it has turned out to be one of: a method artifact (the 16% null), a property of the photographs (the untrained control), a single-seed artifact (the nose), or background-confounded (the eyes). The interpretability evidence is currently silent on which facial features carry identity.

There is one constructive lead. On the clean background, training moves attention to the periphery — fur, ears, head outline — and away from the central features. If that is real it is a biological claim worth testing directly, and the machinery already exists: the occlusion arms in section 09 can mask the periphery instead of the face and measure what performance actually depends on. That is a measurement, not another map.

Section 08

The background shortcut

This was raised as a worry and turned out to be the largest single problem in the project. It is a design flaw in the dataset, not a bug in any code.

Evaluation scores pairs. Each bat was filmed in exactly one video. So a same-bat pair is always two frames of one video — near-identical background — and a different-bat pair never is. "Do these two photographs share a background?" is therefore an almost perfect stand-in for "are these the same bat?", answerable without looking at an animal.

Identity-disjoint splitting does not help. It stops the model memorising which bat is which, but the cue here is within-pair background agreement, which needs no knowledge of any individual. A model that has never seen a test bat can still notice that two of its photographs were taken in the same room.

Concept

A model-free probe, and why the negative controls matter more than the result

The test needs no model. Keep the same test pairs, the same labels and the same metric, and replace only the score: instead of the cosine between two embeddings, use the cosine between two background-colour descriptors, with the face masked out. Whatever ROC-AUC comes back is obtainable without a single face pixel.

The trustworthiness comes from the controls. green paints a flat colour behind every face and random draws a fresh background per image, so neither can carry bat identity — both must return ≈0.50. If they do not, the probe is measuring something else (face leaking through a loose mask, say) and its headline number means nothing.

Section 09

The controls: what survives when the shortcut is removed

Sections 03 and 08 left two loose ends. The background shortcut was shown to be available but not shown to be used. And the image-quality confound was measured but never actually held constant while the models were retrained. Closing both took a second round of 624 runs across seven dataset arms — every one completed clean.

One result confirms what section 08 suspected, in a stronger form than that section could support. The other reverses a reading from section 03.

Terminology

What an "arm" is

Borrowed from clinical trials. An arm is one version of the dataset plus the runs trained on it, where exactly one thing has been changed on purpose and everything else — the model, the loss, the folds, the metric — is held fixed. Comparing two arms therefore isolates that one change.

There are seven here. green, original and random differ only in what sits behind the bat. band keeps only images in a shared resolution range. blur and recrop degrade the mauritius images two different ways. bgonly deletes the face entirely. Same bats throughout.

What the background result means in practice

Each bat was filmed once, so each bat has its own background. The task is "are these two photographs the same animal?" — and a shortcut exists: if the two backgrounds match, answer yes. It gets the right answer most of the time without ever looking at a bat.

Section 08 showed the shortcut is there (a colour histogram, no network, scores 0.74–0.79). This section shows a real model takes it: with the face deleted from every image, an ArcFace still reaches 0.831 and 0.780. So the numbers from the original background are mostly background matching, not face recognition, and they cannot be rescued — one video per bat means no same-bat pair can ever span two different backgrounds. The headline has to come from green, where every image sits on the same flat screen and the background carries no information about which bat it is.

Deleting the face, then training anyway

The design is blunt on purpose. Take every image — training, validation and test alike — find the face with the green-screen matte, grow that region by 24 px so no facial edge survives, and let OpenCV inpaint it: fill the hole by extrapolating the surrounding background inward. There is no face and no face-shaped outline left. Then train an ArcFace from scratch and ask it to verify bats it has never seen.

Concept

Why the fill has to be inpainting, not a flat patch

The earlier ablation painted the face region a single flat colour. That leaves the outline of the face — its silhouette — perfectly intact. Since silhouette is itself a known open confound in this project, a model that scored well on flat-filled images would be uninterpretable: it could be reading the background, or the shape of the hole. Inpainting removes both, so a positive result has only one explanation left.

Holding image quality constant — and the reversal

Section 03 established that mauritius faces are captured about twice as large as rousettus ones, with no overlap at all, and that matching resolution collapsed the separability gap. The natural conclusion was that "rousettus individuals are less distinctive" is substantially a measurement artifact. Testing that properly means degrading the mauritius images and retraining.

The first thing that fell out was that the planned recipe does nothing. The models train at 112 px, and no image in either species is upscaled at 112 — rousettus' smallest face is 178 px. Both species are shrunk down to 112 and both then hold exactly 112 px of face. There is no pixel-count difference left to remove. The gap that does exist is optical: a face filmed small in frame is softer and noisier per unit of face, because focus, motion blur and frame-level JPEG compression all act before anything is cropped.

So three separate controls were built, chosen to fail in different directions:

  • Re-crop — reproduce the cause. Downsample each bat's source frames by its own factor and cut a new crop: literally the same animal filmed further away. Detection runs on the full-resolution frame, so the crop geometry is identical and only the sampled pixels change. Nothing is tuned to hit a target.
  • Blur — target the measured effect. Solve, per bat, for the exact amount of blur that puts its sharpness on the matching rousettus value.
  • Band restriction — no synthesis at all. Keep only images whose native face size falls in the range both species genuinely occupy. Costs six mauritius bats and two rousettus bats, leaving ten each.

These arms also had to move to the random background, which is where the species difference actually lives. Across the 360-run sweep the two species differ on random only — green is flat with rousettus marginally ahead, and original is the arm the shortcut ruins. Controlling the confound on green and original alone would have tested everything except the effect in question.

The defensible statement is a bounded negative: matching image quality changed the random-background species gap by at most 0.043 ROC-AUC across three independent controls.

This does not contradict section 03's measurements, only its interpretation. That analysis measured the geometry of an untrained backbone's embedding space — d′, Fisher ratio, silhouette. This one measures trained open-set verification. Matching resolution flattens the first and not the second, so they are answering different questions, and the recognition question is the one the paper makes claims about.

Why none of these rows carries a p-value

Section 05's power ceiling applies with full force here. This design cannot resolve a difference below 0.440 ROC-AUC, and every gap in the table is around 0.12. So no comparison here can come back significant, whatever the truth is, and reporting one as non-significant would be meaningless. What carries the conclusion instead is that three controls which fail in different directions all agree, and that the one with no free parameters moved nothing.

Two smaller things, and one correction

The rousettus manifest asymmetry turned out to be harmless. The green build had 34 more images than the other two backgrounds, so the published background comparison was not quite comparing like with like. Re-running it on the exact intersection of all three moves nothing: Siamese −0.035, ArcFace −0.012, AdaFace +0.037, every interval containing zero.

The saliency resolution problem from section 07 now has a fix available. A CAM heatmap's grid is the input size divided by the network's output stride, which gave a 10 × 10 grid with 32 px cells — bigger than an eye, so eye-level claims were unsupportable at any input size. Dilating the last ResNet stage instead of striding it halves the output stride, and the trained checkpoints now produce a 20 × 20 grid with 16 px cells at identical parameter count. This removes the obstacle; it does not revisit section 07's conclusions, whose randomisation control still fails.

The correction concerns section 03's own numbers. That battery measured one set of crops; the 360-run sweep trained on another. For mauritius they are byte-identical, but for rousettus they are different — and sharper — images. What this does and does not mean

It does not overturn the central claim. Individual bats really are identifiable far above chance — the six-number descriptor is itself proof of that, at 0.83–0.85.

It does mean "a trained network recognises bat faces" is not supported by these numbers. Three model families, two species, twenty folds each, and none of them demonstrably beats a summary that contains no spatial information at all. Whatever the networks add over global colour is smaller than this design can resolve.

And it is the same shape as section 09's background result, from the same cause. There, no model significantly beat a scorer that never sees a face. Here, no model significantly beats a scorer that sees only six colour statistics. Both follow from one video per bat: anything constant within a clip — the room behind the animal, or the animal's own colour rendition — is a free identity cue, and no identity-disjoint split can remove it.

The open question, and the one thing that would settle it

The six numbers may be measuring something real. Individuals plausibly do differ in coat colour and texture statistics — in which case this is a legitimate biological answer to "what distinguishes individuals", just not the one the project has been arguing for, and not one that needs a neural network. The alternative is a per-video capture signature; grey-world white balancing does not remove the cue (0.777 → 0.778), so it is not simple illumination.

One video per bat makes those two indistinguishable. Separating them needs a second recording session for even a handful of individuals — the same requirement the background leak generates. That is now implied by two independent results rather than one, and it is the single most valuable new data this project could collect.

The pattern worth noticing

Most of the refutations above are method defects that produced confident-looking wrong answers, not surprises in the data. And in every case the thing that caught them was an internal consistency check, never a careful read of the output: densities that must sum to 1 not summing to 1; a confidence interval that did not contain its own point estimate; a negative control that did not return chance; a figure that contradicted its own table.

The practical lesson is to build the consistency check before trusting the number. Every one of these would have shipped otherwise.

Section 11

What is defensible, and what isn't

Separating these two lists is the point of the exercise. The first list is what survives scrutiny; the second is what a reviewer would successfully attack.

Defensible

  • Individual bats are recognisable above chance, for both species, in every condition tested. 18 of 18 cells, q ≤ 0.0032 after correcting for all 18 tests. This is the strongest claim in the project. Note the wording: bats, not bat faces — six global colour numbers reach 0.83–0.85 on their own, so recognisability is established while its mechanism is not.
  • Background matters, and in a consistent order. original > green > random, across both species and all three model families — though section 09 shows the original end of that ordering is substantially the background shortcut rather than better recognition.
  • Bat-specific training moves attribution off the eyes and nose, toward the periphery. 38 of 40 models, both species, both backgrounds, q ≤ 0.007. Pre-registered analysis, and the one saliency claim that survives its own controls.
  • The two datasets are not comparable. 11 of 12 quality measures differ; effective resolution separates the species completely (δ = 1.00, no overlap at all).
  • Matching resolution substantially shrinks the apparent species gap in intrinsic separability — while matching sample sizes changes nothing. So a large share of the "species effect" is a photography effect.
  • The green background is the only clean arm, and both species are well above chance on it (mauritius AdaFace 0.854, rousettus AdaFace 0.829). This is where the headline belongs.

Not defensible — do not claim these

  • That any model family beats another. Zero of 18 comparisons survive the correlated-fold correction. The ranking is suggestive only.
  • That mauritius bats are intrinsically more identifiable. Only 2 of 9 cells differ, both on random background, and all of it is confounded by resolution.
  • That the species datasets are equivalent in quality. The opposite is established. No measure passed the equivalence test.
  • That more held-out bats give a more reliable estimate. Measured, and false in this data.
  • Any per-fold permutation p-value quoted as a point estimate when it sits on the floor. 290 of 360 are upper bounds.
  • Anything conditioned on the original background. Background colour alone reaches 0.74–0.79 on the same pairs; no model significantly beats it there.
  • That either model learned to look at the eyes, or that the species differ in where they look. An untrained network reproduces both, and the pre-registered 40-model test found the species difference does not replicate on a clean background — the nose version of it reversed outright. Training in fact moves attribution off the eyes.
  • That the networks recognise faces at all, as opposed to colour. No model demonstrably beats a six-number colour summary with no spatial content, across three families, two species and twenty folds.
  • Which facial features carry individual identity. Every measurement that appeared to answer this turned out to be a method artifact, a property of the photographs, a single-seed artifact, or background-confounded. The interpretability evidence is silent on it — which is a statement about the evidence, not about the bats.

The two things that would most improve the science next

  1. Run the resolution-matched arm. Everything about the species question hinges on it, and it is constructible today: the shared resolution band is 298–623 px, containing about 60% of each species' images, leaving 10 of 16 mauritius and 11 of 12 rousettus bats with at least 10 usable images each.
  2. Film each individual in more than one setting. This is the only real fix for the background shortcut — with one video per bat, no same-bat pair can span two backgrounds, so the leak is a property of the collection and not of the analysis.
  3. Report effect sizes against the 0.44 ceiling, not against p = 0.05. "Add more bats" is not actionable (there are none), so the honest move is to state the detectable limit and quote effects with intervals.

Traps — these produce wrong answers without failing

  • Any saliency output from before 25 Aug 2026 is suspect. What to do next, in order

  • Collect a second recording session for even three to five individuals.   • Re-baseline every claim against the six numbers, not against chance. One script, about a minute, gives the paired per-fold margin with an interval.
  • Rewrite the species and saliency sections. Both conclusions flipped: the species gap is real and not a resolution artifact, and the eye-attention account does not survive its controls.
  • Optional: re-run the ROI saliency on green checkpoints at 320 px. The machinery is built; only the checkpoints need retaining.

Method

Five practices that produced every correction here

1 · Pre-register before the data exists. Two hypotheses were registered and both failed; a third reversed. Without registration the nose result would have been written up from the single seed it was found in. Commit order is the audit trail.

2 · Every arm needs a control, and the control gates the result. This caught five defects — including two introduced during this work, both recorded as amendments rather than quietly re-run.

3 · Quote against the right null. Not chance — the six-number baseline. An area-share null and a chance null have each produced a false claim in this project.

4 · Effect size, interval and the detectable minimum, never a bare p-value. Below 0.440 ROC-AUC, "not significant" means undetectable, not equal.

5 · Interpretability needs many models. Going from one model to ten flipped the sign of the primary effect; per-fold contrasts ranged +0.046 to −0.586.
