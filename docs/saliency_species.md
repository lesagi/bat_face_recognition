# Where does each species' model look?

Answers reviewer comment 3: *"in the saliency — smooth the saliency with a kernel
to show what each species focuses on."*

**Short answer, and it is not the one an earlier draft of this document gave.**
Both species' models put attribution on the eyes — but so does an **untrained
network of the same architecture**, and the species *difference* survives
randomisation almost unchanged. The eye-attention finding is therefore mostly a
property of the images, not of anything the models learned. What is genuinely
learning-dependent is that training moves the two species in **opposite
directions**: toward the eyes for mauritius, away from them for rousettus.

Generated by `scripts/species_saliency_maps.py`. Raw output:
`outputs/saliency/roi_stats*.json`; figures under `outputs/saliency/figures/`.

## The randomisation control, and why it is decisive

Adebayo et al. (2018): re-run the whole analysis on an untrained network of the
same architecture. Saliency maps look persuasive even for models that have
learned nothing, so any claim about *what a model learned* must clear this bar.

Measured on the same 112 images, IG at 320 px:

| | trained | untrained | attributable to learning |
|---|---|---|---|
| mauritius eye density | 1.669 | **1.771** | nothing — untrained is higher |
| rousettus eye density | 0.911 | **1.322** | training moves it *away* |
| mauritius peak on eye | 82.8% | **64.1%** | +18.7 pp |
| rousettus peak on eye | 14.6% | **47.9%** | −33.3 pp |
| species delta, eye density | +0.98 | **+0.94** | approximately nothing |

Two conclusions:

1. **The correct baseline is 64%, not 16%.** An untrained network peaks on an eye
   in 64% of mauritius images, because gradient attribution lands on
   high-contrast structure and eyes are dark spots on a lighter face. Quoting
   "81% versus a 16% chance rate" compares against the wrong null.
2. **The species difference is not a learned difference.** Cliff's delta is +0.94
   untrained against +0.98 trained, both p < 0.0001. It is a property of the
   images — most plausibly the resolution confound again: mauritius faces occupy
   about twice the pixels, so their eyes are larger, sharper and more attractive
   to any gradient method. See `docs/quality_parity.md`.

### What does survive

The trained-minus-untrained contrast *within* each species, which has opposite
signs: mauritius attention moves **toward** the eyes (64% -> 83%) and rousettus
**away** from them (48% -> 15%). That is learning-dependent and worth pursuing,
but it is a different claim from the original one and needs its own test rather
than inheriting the earlier p-values.

### The earlier control was broken, and passed vacuously

The first version overwrote weights in place and zeroed every 1-D parameter —
which includes each BatchNorm scale gamma. With gamma = 0 the BatchNorm output is
identically zero, so the network emitted nothing and every map came back empty.
That was reported as "100% degenerate, nothing survives, control passed". It
demonstrated a broken model, not a learning-dependent explanation.

The tell was visible and missed: trained IG produced **0%** degenerate maps while
the randomised arm produced **100%** — and IG has no ReLU gate, which is
precisely why it removed the degenerate maps to begin with. Only a dead network
could do that. The control now builds the model at its default initialisation
(gamma = 1) so it runs normally with only the training missing.

## Why Integrated Gradients replaced Grad-CAM

Grad-CAM had two problems here, and the second was biasing the species comparison.

**It silently discarded a third of the data, unevenly.** `embedding_gradcam`
returns an all-zero map when ReLU leaves no positive channel contribution: 20% of
mauritius images and **46%** of rousettus. Those were excluded, so the species
comparison rested on 80% of one species against 54% of the other —
differential attrition, which is a bias and not merely lost precision.

**The cause is the target, not the model.** The first hypothesis — that all-zero
maps track a weak top-class cosine — is *false*: the cosine is statistically
identical for degenerate and usable maps (p = 0.84 mauritius, 0.92 rousettus;
pooled point-biserial r = +0.08, p = 0.26). The real mechanism is that Grad-CAM
weights each channel by its spatially-averaged gradient, which assumes the target
**grows with activation magnitude**. The `class_logit` target is a cosine between
L2-normalised vectors — completely scale-invariant — so its gradient has no
radial component and the pooled weights can come out net-negative, whereupon ReLU
erases the map. Swapping to the scale-*dependent* `emb_mag` target confirms it:

| Grad-CAM target | mauritius degenerate | rousettus degenerate |
|---|---|---|
| `class_logit` (cosine, scale-invariant) | 19.8% | 45.8% |
| `emb_mag` (‖emb‖², scale-dependent) | **0.0%** | **0.0%** |

**Integrated Gradients has no ReLU gate and no grid.** It attributes at full input
resolution and scored **127/127 and 96/96** images — zero attrition — while
keeping the class-discriminative `class_logit` target. It is therefore the
primary method here, with SmoothGrad averaging (20 integration steps × 12 noisy
copies) to control per-pixel noise.

## The measurement only works at 320 px

This is the load-bearing methodological point. Grad-CAM's spatial resolution is
the backbone's final conv grid, which for ResNet50 is `input_edge / 32`:

| model input | conv grid | cell size on the crop | eye ROI resolvable? |
|---|---|---|---|
| 112 px (the tuned default) | **4 × 4** | 56 × 56 px | **no** — one cell is larger than the whole eye ROI |
| 320 px | **10 × 10** | 32 × 32 px | yes |

At 112 px every density came out near 1.0 for both species, and the per-pixel SD
map showed a visible 4×4 blocking artifact. That was not a finding about bats; it
was the map being too coarse to say anything. Re-running at 320 px separates the
species cleanly. Any ROI-level saliency claim in the write-up must state the
input resolution it was measured at.

(This independently reproduces the earlier observation that a 320 px 10×10
Grad-CAM localises the eyes sharply *with the class-logit target* — so the
convergence is a check on both results.)

## Method

- **Target**: `class_logit` — cosine to the top *predicted* identity
  (`argmax` over the normalised head weights). Not the ground-truth class: the
  test identities are held out, so in an open-set evaluation there is no such
  label. This is why `bat_interpretability.GradCAMAdapter` is not used here — its
  `"class"` target requires an explicit `target_class`.
- **Group map**: mean and per-pixel SD over every image, in the aligned frame.
  The crops are eye-aligned and mask-centred, so pixel coordinates are
  anatomically comparable and averaging is meaningful. Smoothing is applied
  **across images** (σ = 6 px, stated in every caption) — smoothing across images
  adds information; smoothing one map only hides its coarseness.
- **ROIs** from `face_pose.pt` run directly on the aligned crop (already a tight,
  mask-centred square — the distribution that model was trained on). Eyes and
  nose are 0.16·edge discs; nose ∩ eyes is assigned to eyes so the three regions
  partition the frame and the mass fractions sum to 1.
- **Density = mass / area**, so 1.0 means "this region got exactly its area
  share". This is the interpretable form: a raw mass of 0.16 for the eyes sounds
  small but is exactly chance.
- **Unit of analysis is the identity** — per-image values are averaged within a
  bat before comparing species, because images of one bat come from one video.
- **Degenerate maps are excluded, not scored as zero.** `embedding_gradcam`
  returns an all-zero map when ReLU leaves no positive channel contribution.
  Counting those as "density 0" both mis-measures them and drags the mean toward
  zero — it is what initially made rousettus' densities sum to 0.40 when they must
  sum to 1.0 by construction.

## Result — Integrated Gradients, ArcFace, original background, 320 px input

All 223 images scored; nothing dropped. p-values are Benjamini–Hochberg corrected
across the three regions.

| region | area share | mauritius | rousettus | Cliff's δ | q |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| eyes | 0.161 | **1.651** | 0.921 | **+0.91** | **0.00009** |
| nose | 0.078 | 1.145 | 0.728 | +0.85 | 0.00015 |
| periphery | 0.761 | 0.848 | 1.045 | −0.91 | 0.00009 |

Pointing game — where the single hottest pixel lands:

| species | eyes | nose | periphery |
|---|---|---|---|
| chance (area) | 16.1% | 7.8% | 76.1% |
| **mauritius** | **81.1%** | 6.3% | 12.6% |
| **rousettus** | 17.7% | 0.0% | 82.3% |

### Reading it — against the correct baseline

Read on its own this table says mauritius peaks on an eye in 4 of 5 images and
rousettus sits at chance. **Do not read it on its own.** The untrained network
above scores 64% and 48% on the same measure, so the area-share column is not
the relevant null and these numbers overstate what the models learned.

The statistics (delta = ±0.91, q <= 0.00015) are correct as a description of the
trained models' attribution, and they are reproduced by an untrained network at
delta = +0.94 — which is what makes them uninterpretable as evidence about
learning.

### Superseded: the same measurement with Grad-CAM

Kept for comparison, because the difference between these two tables is the
argument for using IG.

| region | mauritius | rousettus | Cliff's δ | p (raw) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| eyes | 1.574 | 1.005 | +0.47 | 0.046 → q ≈ 0.14 |
| nose | 1.266 | 0.906 | +0.43 | 0.064 |
| periphery | 0.852 | 1.008 | −0.45 | 0.051 |

Pointing game: mauritius 33.3% / rousettus 16.8% on eyes. Same direction, roughly
half the effect, and not significant after correction — because a third of the
images had been silently discarded and the 10×10 grid could not resolve a
36 px eye region.

That converges with the existing silhouette control, which found that nearly all
of the rousettus signal survives removing facial texture and is carried by the
head-crop *outline*. A model relying on outline rather than features is exactly a
model whose saliency is spatially uniform over the face. Two independent methods,
same conclusion.

### Randomisation control

See *The randomisation control, and why it is decisive* at the top of this
document. The claim previously made here — that 100% of randomised maps are
degenerate and therefore nothing survives — was an artifact of a broken control.

## Limitations — state these alongside the result

Two limitations from the Grad-CAM version are now **resolved** and are listed
here only so the change is traceable: significance was marginal (q ≈ 0.14, now
0.00009) and the degenerate-map rate differed by species (20% vs 46%, now 0% vs
0%). Both were artifacts of the method, not of the data.

What remains:

- **Mismatched checkpoint criterion.** mauritius used `best_model_roc_auc.pt`;
  rousettus at 320 px never improved val ROC-AUC and so saved no such
  checkpoint — `resolve_ckpt` fell back to `best_model_f1.pt`. That rousettus
  fails to train at 320 px is itself consistent with the resolution study
  (320 px does not help, and ~49% of rousettus crops are upscaled there), but it
  means the two arms were not selected on the same criterion. This is now the
  single biggest caveat on the comparison.
- **Trained on the leaking background.** Both checkpoints are original-background
  models, and `docs/background_leakage.md` shows that arm is substantially
  solvable from the scene alone. A model that partly scores pairs on background
  may distribute its attention differently from one forced onto the face, so this
  measurement should be repeated on `green` before being leaned on.
- **One model family, one background.** ArcFace on original background only.
  AdaFace and the other backgrounds are a `--model` / re-run away but were not
  measured.
- The eye/nose ROI radii (0.16·edge) are a judgement call, not calibrated
  anatomy. The densities scale with that choice; the *ratio* between species does
  not.
- IG is ~50× slower than Grad-CAM here (240 forward/backward passes per image),
  which is why the sample is capped per identity rather than exhaustive.
